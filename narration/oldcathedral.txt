## Welcome to the original
Okay. Stop walking. You're standing next to a celebrity, and it's wearing brick. This is Saint Patrick's Old Cathedral, and yes, "old" is doing a lot of work there. Before the famous Saint Patrick's on Fifth Avenue, before the spires and the tourists, this was New York's cathedral. It was built between eighteen-oh-nine and eighteen-fifteen, when this spot was near the edge of the city. Up here you'd have been looking at farms. Today you're looking at a pizza place. Progress.

## The wall with a job
Now look at that tall brick wall running along Mott Street. It's not there to look pretty. In the eighteen-thirties and eighteen-forties, anti-Catholic mobs were a real threat in New York, and parishioners stood guard to protect this church. In eighteen-forty-four, Bishop John Hughes warned the city that if a single Catholic church burned, New York would become "a second Moscow." That's a reference to the city that burned in eighteen-twelve. The churches stayed standing. So when you walk past this wall, think of it less as a fence and more as a bodyguard.

## Fire, and a second life
Look up at the front, above the tall pointed window. See that small round window near the peak? It's watching you back. In eighteen-sixty-six a fire gutted the cathedral, and it was rebuilt and reopened two years later. In the app's "Then" photos you can see the older version, drawn around eighteen-thirty with a steeple it doesn't have anymore. Same bones, new life. Very New York.

## A saint in the churchyard
Behind that wall is one of the only old churchyards left in Manhattan. For a long time it held Pierre Toussaint, a Haitian-born man who arrived here enslaved, became free, and grew into one of the city's best-known hairdressers. He spent his money caring for orphans, the sick and strangers. He's now on the path to sainthood, and in nineteen-ninety his remains were moved to the crypt at the Fifth Avenue cathedral. Not bad for a man who started out doing hair downtown.

## Lights, camera, baptism
And now, the moment the film fans have been waiting for. Inside this church, Francis Ford Coppola filmed the baptism scene in The Godfather. Organ music, holy water, and a lot of very bad business happening across town at the same time. The baby being baptized? Coppola's daughter, Sofia, who grew up to direct films of her own. And a few blocks from here, a kid named Martin Scorsese grew up and served as an altar boy at this church. He later put it in Mean Streets. Two of the greatest New York filmmakers, one brick church.

## Before you go
This is still a working church, and since twenty-ten it's officially a basilica. So if you step inside, keep it quiet and respectful. Otherwise, enjoy it from the sidewalk. Take one last look at that wall, and that little round window. Then follow Mott Street onward. Your next plot twist is waiting.
