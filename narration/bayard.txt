## The only one
Find the top of Crosby Street and face the white building at the end of it. Perfect. This is the Bayard-Condict Building, finished in eighteen-ninety-nine. It's the only building in all of New York designed by Louis Sullivan. If that name is new to you, here's the shortcut: Sullivan taught Frank Lloyd Wright, who called him his "Lieber Meister," his beloved master. Chicago got dozens of Sullivan buildings. New York got exactly one. You're looking at it.

## Form follows function
Sullivan is the guy who coined "form follows function." It's one of the most quoted lines in architecture, from his eighteen-ninety-six essay about tall office buildings. He wanted a skyscraper to look tall and proud of it. So follow those thin white piers with your eyes, from the street all the way up, thirteen stories. Notice how nothing stops your gaze? That's the point. Under that white skin is a steel frame, and Sullivan wanted you to feel it rising.

## The lace
Now look between the windows. That's not stone, it's terra-cotta: baked clay, molded into leaves, seeds, swirls and flowers. Up close it looks less like a building and more like lace, or icing. Sullivan believed a building should be as alive as a plant, with ornament growing out of the structure like leaves from a stem. Some people in eighteen-ninety-nine thought it was too much. Today people come from all over the world just to stand where you're standing.

## Spot the angels
Okay, here's your challenge. Look all the way up, to the very top. Under the cornice, between the arches, there are six winged figures stretching out their arms. Can you find all six? Here's the gossip: Sullivan reportedly didn't want them. The client, Silas Alden Condict, insisted, and when the client pays, the angels stay. So six figures have watched over Bleecker Street for more than a hundred years, possibly against the architect's wishes. Give them a wave. They've earned it.

## Caught on camera
In nineteen-seventy, a photographer named Cervin Robinson climbed in close to these details for the Historic American Buildings Survey, the national record of buildings worth saving. His photos are in the app's "Then" section, so you can see those angels up close without a ladder. A few years later the building was protected as a landmark. And some of its original storefront capitals, removed in nineteen-sixty-four, now live at the Brooklyn Museum. The building literally has pieces in a museum.

## Before you go
Sullivan later wrote a book about his life called The Autobiography of an Idea. That's a pretty good description of this building, too: one big idea, standing in white clay on a narrow street. Bleecker is busy, so mind the traffic. Next stop is just around the corner.
